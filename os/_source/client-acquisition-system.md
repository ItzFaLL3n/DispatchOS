# Client Acquisition System

How the free build becomes a paying client, and how a paying client becomes a growth-system client.

This layers Alex Hormozi's offer and lead frameworks onto the ten-phase intake playbook that's already working, and takes the ascension model from the Stone Method notes. It doesn't replace `prospect-intake-playbook.md` — that stays the tactical script. This is the strategy underneath it.

---

## Rule zero: where Hormozi gets overruled

Hormozi's material is built for offers that can promise a measurable business outcome and guarantee it. A lot of it is genuinely useful here. Some of it directly contradicts rules that exist for good reasons. Where they conflict, the existing rules win — every time, no exceptions.

| Hormozi says | What applies here instead |
|---|---|
| Lead with the dream outcome, make a big claim | **Never promise leads, calls, or rankings.** Lead with the outcome *of the deliverable* ("a site that looks like your operation"), not a business result you don't control |
| Stack a guarantee that reverses all risk | Service guarantees only: no contract, cancel anytime, 14-day adjustment window, no hidden fees. Never a results guarantee |
| Use scarcity and urgency aggressively | Only genuine operational caps. "Taking two builds this month" because that's actually true |
| Follow up relentlessly until they buy or die | **One light follow-up, then let it sit.** A stated deferral is respected, not chased |
| More offers, more often | **One ask per message.** Never bundle |

Everything below is filtered through that table already.

## Part 1 — The offer, built properly

### The value equation, applied

Hormozi's framing: perceived value goes up with the **dream outcome** and the **likelihood they'll actually get it**, and down with **time delay** and **effort required from them**. Most people try to raise value by inflating the promise. The lever that actually works here is the bottom of the equation.

Score the current offer honestly:

| Lever | Current state | Move |
|---|---|---|
| Dream outcome | "look as credible online as you are in the field" | Fine. Don't inflate it into a lead promise |
| Likelihood of achievement | **Weak.** He's a stranger on Facebook. ProLift's owner literally flagged the outreach as a possible scam | **This is the lever.** Proof beats persuasion — see below |
| Time delay | Days from yes to a live site | Strong. Lean on it |
| Effort & sacrifice | A few photos, a few questions, no money, no commitment | Very strong. This is why the free build works at all |

**So the whole game is likelihood.** Two things move it and nothing else does:

1. **Delivered work you can show.** Tyler's live site. ProLift's live site. A screenshot of a real hauling company's real page beats any sentence about what you'd build.
2. **Doing exactly what you said, on time, with no surprise ask attached.** The zero-ask check-in isn't politeness — it's the single highest-leverage credibility move in the whole sequence, because nobody else does it.

**Action:** build a one-screen proof page (a Dispatch OS page, not client-facing) holding live URLs and before/after screenshots for every delivered site. It goes in the DM at the exact moment skepticism shows up, and nowhere else.

### The free build is a lead magnet, and it has to behave like one

A good lead magnet solves one narrow problem *completely* and, in solving it, makes the *next* problem visible. That's not a trick — it's what happens naturally when you actually fix something.

The free site does this on its own:

- **Problem it solves completely:** they have no web presence, or a bad one. Now they have a real one they own.
- **Problem it reveals:** it's sitting on a `.vercel.app` subdomain. It isn't going to show up when someone in their town searches for junk removal. Estimate requests land in a Gmail inbox with no history and no tracking.

**This is the honest bridge to paid, and it must never be manufactured.** Don't cripple the free site to create a gap. Don't hide the subdomain limitation. Build the best free site you can, and the gap appears by itself — because it's a real gap.

The rule: **let them find it, don't point at it.** If the Phase 8 check-in comes back "no calls yet," that's expected on a subdomain and it's the moment the retainer conversation makes sense — not because you engineered it, but because the honest answer to "why no calls" is "it's not on a real domain yet."

### The offer stack

Hormozi's stacking logic: name each component, and each component should map to a problem they'd otherwise have to solve themselves. The current $39/$49 tier already contains real components — they're just not named, which means they're not being valued.

**What $39/mo actually includes, itemized:**
- Site stays live and hosted
- Ongoing updates when their business changes
- Separate service pages instead of one page
- Ongoing SEO work so the site can actually be found
- Job and customer tracking dashboard
- Estimate requests kept in one place instead of scattered in email

**$49/mo:** same, plus the domain handled entirely — registration, DNS, renewal, none of it their problem.

Right now the pitch says "hosted and maintained." That undersells six things down to one. **Name them.** Not as a hype stack — as a plain list, when he asks what he's paying for.

### Two options, never yes-or-no

The $39/$49 split is already doing something important and it's worth naming: it changes the question from *"do I pay?"* to *"which one?"* Keep it. Never collapse it to a single number.

The genuine difference is worth being straight about: self-managing a domain means registering it, pointing DNS records at Vercel, waiting on propagation, and remembering to renew it before it lapses. That's a real thing people get wrong, and it's a real reason to pay $10 more. Say it plainly. Don't dramatize it.

## Part 2 — Getting leads (Core Four, applied to a Facebook-only business)

Hormozi's four ways to get anyone: warm outreach, cold outreach, posting content, running ads. Bruce currently runs about one and a half of them.

| Channel | Current state | Next move |
|---|---|---|
| **Warm outreach** | Not running | **Biggest untapped one.** Tyler is delighted and paying nothing. Ask him for one introduction to another junk removal guy he knows. That's a warm lead with a live reference attached, which is the single strongest lead type available |
| **Cold outreach** | Running — FB DMs to page owners | Constrained by account health. Two aged accounts warming up. Keep it slow |
| **Free content** | Running — FB group posts, two-version format | Working. This is the main engine. Scale by working more groups, not by posting more in the same ones |
| **Paid ads** | Not running | Correct. Don't start until 2–3 clients are on the same standardized system and close rate is known. Ads amplify whatever is already happening — including a broken funnel |

### The referral ask (do this)

The highest-ROI unused move in the whole business. Tyler got a free site, loves it, and has cost nothing. He also knows other junk removal operators.

Timing: at Phase 8, after the zero-ask check-in lands well — never in the same message. Separate touch, separate day.

> *"random question, you know any other guys in the area running something similar? been doing a couple of these builds and would rather work off someone knowing someone than cold messaging strangers all day"*

One ask. No incentive attached. No follow-up if he doesn't bite.

### Volume math

Track it so decisions stop being guesses:

```
posts run → replies → intakes started → sites delivered → retainer closes
```

Right now the pipeline is roughly: 5 tracked prospects, 2 delivered, 0 paying. **That's not a bad funnel — it's a funnel that hasn't reached the monetization step yet.** Two clients are still ahead of Phase 8. One deferred honestly. The conclusion isn't "change the offer." It's "run more volume into a sequence that hasn't finished executing once."

Do not redesign the offer before three clients have completed Phase 9.

## Part 3 — The conversion moments, phase by phase

The existing playbook is the script. This is what's actually happening underneath at each gate, and what has to be true to move forward.

### Phases 1–5 — Intake
**Underneath:** effort reduction. Every question asked is friction. Every question *not* asked because you checked their Facebook page first is a small deposit of credibility.
**Gate to advance:** you can build the site without asking anything else.
**Money rule:** pricing does not exist yet. Not hinted at, not foreshadowed.

### Phase 6 — Mid-build check-in
**Underneath:** likelihood-of-achievement. This is the first moment they see you're real. It worked on Tyler and it's worth repeating every time.
**Gate:** he's seen progress and reacted.

### Phase 7 — Delivery
**Underneath:** the reciprocity moment. Its power is entirely destroyed by an attached ask.
**Gate:** delivered plain, nothing attached.
**Money rule:** absolutely nothing about money. This is the rule most likely to get broken under excitement and it's the most expensive one to break.

### Phase 8 — Zero-ask check-in
**Underneath:** this is where the relationship is actually made. Skip it and the free build becomes a one-time favor from a stranger.
**Gate:** he replied. That's it.

> *"hows the site treating you so far, any calls come through yet?"*

**What his answer tells you:**
- *"a couple came in"* → he's getting value. Retainer is easy. Go to Phase 9.
- *"nothing yet"* → expected on a subdomain. **Do not explain that in this message.** Say something normal, let it sit a few days, then open Phase 9 — where the domain explanation is a natural answer, not a defensive one.
- *silence* → one light follow-up. Then leave it.

### Phase 8.5 — The bridge (this is the money phase)
Not in the original playbook. It's the moment between the check-in and the pitch, and it's where the conversion is actually won or lost.

**What has to be true before you pitch:**
- [ ] Site delivered and live
- [ ] Zero-ask check-in sent and answered
- [ ] At least a few days have passed since delivery
- [ ] PayPal plan for the tier already created and the link ready to send
- [ ] Nothing was asked of him between delivery and now

**The bridge move:** don't invent a reason to pitch. Wait for one of these, all of which come from him:
- he asks about Google, ranking, or being found
- he mentions a missed call or a lead he lost
- he asks about a domain or "making it official"
- he asks how to see estimate requests
- he mentions being busier

Any of those is an open door. HeartLand already raised Google ranking unprompted — that's a door, held until after delivery.

If no door appears within about a week, open Phase 9 anyway. Once. Plainly.

### Phase 9 — The retainer offer
The single highest-stakes message in the sequence. One ask, two options, no pressure.

> *"hey, wanted to run something by you — i can keep the site hosted and maintained for $39/month if you handle the domain, or $49 if you want me to handle that too. no contract, cancel whenever."*

**If he asks what he gets for it,** that's the moment to name the six components — plainly, as a list, not as a pitch.

**If he asks why it costs anything,** the honest answer: it's not the hosting, it's the ongoing work — updates, service pages, SEO, keeping the dashboard running. Don't pretend infrastructure costs $39.

**If he defers:** soft yes-later. Acknowledge once. Set `doNotPitchUntil` in Dispatch OS. Don't re-pitch. Tyler's window opens ~September 18 and it does not open early.

**If he says no outright:** the site stays his. Say so and mean it. That sentence is worth more later than the $39 was.

### Phase 9b — The domain trigger
Underused. The domain is the thing they emotionally want — it's what makes the business feel real to them, more than hosting ever will.

When someone hesitates on the retainer but keeps circling the domain, that's the buying signal. The $49 tier exists precisely for that person: they want the domain, they don't want to deal with DNS. Let them buy the thing they actually want.

### Phase 10 — Growth System ascension
Full detail in `growth-system-upgrade-offer.md`. Two things to remember here:

**Timing:** 2+ months on the retainer by default. Earlier only on a genuine organic signal, never forced.

**Anchoring:** the cost comparison table in that doc is the anchor — GBP management alone runs $150–300/mo standalone, review tools $150–300/mo, missed-call text-back $50–100/mo. Stacked, Tier 3's components pass $799 easily. **That's a real comparison, so use it — but only if he pushes on price.** Leading with it turns a plain offer into a sales pitch, which is the one thing that's never worked with these guys.

## Part 4 — Ascension (the Stone Method layer)

The Stone notes make one structural argument worth taking seriously: **stop selling pieces.** Hosting at $39, GBP as a line item, review agent as another line — that's five invoices from one guy, and junk removal operators don't want that. They want one thing that works and one bill.

**The path:**

```
free build          $0      →  proof, trust, a live site they own
hosting retainer    $39/49  →  the first yes. Small on purpose
Growth Foundation   $197    →  hosting absorbed, GBP + CRM + missed-call text-back
Growth              $297    →  + review agent + lead follow-up
Full System         $799    →  + chat widget + full pipeline
```

The $39 is not where the money is. **The $39 exists to convert someone from a free-build recipient into a paying client** — a category change that makes every later offer a different conversation. Going from $0 to $39 is the hard step. Going from $39 to $197 is a much smaller psychological move than $0 to $197 would ever be.

**Standardization is what makes this scale.** Once 2–3 junk removal clients run the same setup, stop building custom and start deploying a template with a different business name on it. Same niche, new cities. That's the Stone model, and it's also what makes the Phase 4 site-builder agent in the build spec worth building.

### Ascension triggers — watch for these in client notes

The pipeline auditor should surface any client whose notes contain one:

| Signal | Points to |
|---|---|
| "how do I show up on Google" | GBP → Foundation tier |
| "I missed a call while I was on a job" | missed-call text-back → Foundation |
| "how do I get more reviews" | review agent → Growth |
| "how do I keep track of jobs" | CRM/dashboard → already promised, deliver it |
| "we're getting busier" | any tier — the money objection just weakened |

## Part 5 — The scoreboard

Track these in Dispatch OS. Four numbers, reviewed monthly. Don't add more.

1. **Reply rate** — replies ÷ outreach touches. Tells you if the post/DM copy works.
2. **Intake→delivery rate** — sites delivered ÷ intakes started. Tells you if you're losing people mid-build. Total Property Service is currently at risk here.
3. **Retainer close rate** — paying ÷ delivered. **The number that matters most and is currently zero, from a sample of two.** Not a signal yet. It becomes a signal at n=3.
4. **MRR** — the only number that compounds.

### Live experiment
Total Property Service got pricing disclosed early, ahead of the standard sequence, because they flagged tight finances. That's a deliberate deviation and the outcome should be recorded either way. If early disclosure converts as well or better, the playbook changes. If it doesn't, the Phase 9 rule holds and now it's held for a reason rather than a hunch. Don't run a second early-disclosure test until this one resolves.

## Part 6 — The one-page version

- The free site is the lead magnet. Build the best one you can; the gap it reveals is real and doesn't need manufacturing.
- Likelihood-of-achievement is the weak lever. Fix it with delivered proof, not better copy.
- Delivery with nothing attached is the whole strategy. Protect it.
- The zero-ask check-in is not optional. It's the step that makes this a relationship.
- Two options, never yes-or-no.
- A deferral is a yes-later. Set the date, then leave it alone.
- Ask Tyler for one referral. It's free and it's the strongest lead available.
- $39 isn't the money. It's the category change that makes $197 possible.
- Never promise a result. Ever. Not once, not to close, not under pressure.
